Sources & credits

This atlas is assembled from published scholarship, open historical-GIS datasets, and freely-licensed maps and images, each credited below. Facts were checked against the Wikipedia article each entry cites, in English or Japanese.

Every entry on the map carries its own citation. All 445 of them (battles, castles, temples and shrines, events, people, historic roads and march routes) were checked one by one against their source article, and each info panel links to the article it rests on under "Further reading". That verification pass corrected real errors: misattributed commanders, lunar dates converted a month or a year wrong, markers placed tens of kilometres from the actual site, and several popular claims that the sources do not support.

Narrative & historical sources

The history (what happened, who, when, and why) is drawn primarily from:

  • The Cambridge History of Japan, Volumes 1–6 (Cambridge University Press), and The New Cambridge History of Japan, Volumes 2–3, the principal scholarly narrative across all eras.
  • The Chronicle of Lord Nobunaga (Shinchō Kōki), trans. J. S. A. Elisonas & J. P. Lamers (Brill), a primary source for the Oda era.
  • The Tale of the Heike, the classic account of the Genpei War.
  • The ancient chronicles, the Kojiki (712) and the Nihon Shoki (Nihongi, 720), Japan's earliest histories, for the mythological and early-imperial periods.
  • The Hōjō Godaiki; Kaempfer's Japan (Tokugawa culture observed); Samurai Warfare; The Founding of the Kamakura Shogunate; and other specialist works.
  • English and Japanese Wikipedia, used throughout for verification. Most entries cite the English edition.

Coordinates & geographic data

  • The Wikipedia article each entry cites, usually the English edition. For most battles the coordinate was taken from that article. An audit of the 173 battles the atlas held in July 2026 found that 94 coordinates match the cited article's own to within 15 metres, 50 were placed by hand because the article gave none, and 29 differ from it, some of them deliberately.
  • Corrections against independent references: Japanese Wikipedia, the national register of cultural properties (文化遺産オンライン), and the Geospatial Information Authority of Japan. These moved several markers that had been tens of metres to several kilometres from the site their label names.

Not every coordinate is the same kind of claim. Some markers sit on a castle or temple whose ground survives; many sit on a place name whose battlefield has no known extent; a few sit on one of several rival candidate sites, or stand in for a whole war or campaign. Where a location is uncertain the entry says so in its location line or its text.

How sure is each place? Reading the battle markers

A map draws every point with the same confidence, although the evidence behind them differs enormously. Each battle therefore records its evidence in separate fields, and the marker and the info panel ("How we know where it was") show them.

  • The place. Site secured (filled marker): the site survives or is secured, as a castle or temple site, an excavated or designated historic site, or an unmistakable landform. Place name only (hollow marker): the name is known but not the extent of the fighting. Site disputed (dashed marker): rival sites are proposed; they are numbered on the map when the entry is open, each with its own source.
  • Did it happen? A marker with a question mark is an event whose occurrence itself is doubted. That is a different question from where it was, and is kept apart from it.
  • What the marker stands for. One engagement, a war or campaign over a wider area, or several engagements combined in one entry. Where a sourced boundary exists, the area is shaded when the entry is open: whole provinces or districts from the CODH boundary dataset, or, for the Honnō-ji, the one block that excavation has confirmed. Whole provinces are only an approximation of where fighting took place.
  • Coordinate. Where the number came from: the Wikipedia article the entry cites (English or Japanese), Wikidata, the place-name search of the Geospatial Information Authority of Japan, the CODH gazetteer of historical place names, the castle or temple layer of this atlas, or placed by hand where no published coordinate could be identified. Sources were matched in November 2026: a marker counts as copied when it lies within 15 metres of the source.
  • Basis of the coding. Verified: checked against a source outside Wikipedia, such as the national register of cultural properties or an excavation report. From the sources: follows from what the entry and its cited sources say. Default: assigned by a rule and not yet checked one by one; most "place name only" markers are of this kind, so their number is an upper limit.

The scheme follows a written coding procedure developed for an audit of this atlas's battles, applied to all of them, with the battles added after the original audit coded by its mechanical rules until they are reviewed individually. Battles fought at a castle or temple in those layers share a single point with it, and the data check refuses to publish if the two ever differ.

Map data & licensing

Old-province boundaries (旧国)

The historical province outlines are from the Center for Open Data in the Humanities (CODH) 旧国・旧郡境界データセット (Historical Administrative Boundary Dataset), simplified for the web. Licensed CC BY-NC 4.0.

Important: the CODH boundary data is non-commercial (CC BY-NC). This atlas may be freely shared and used for education and study with attribution, but it, and any work reusing the province data, must remain non-commercial.

Old districts (旧郡) and other shapes

District outlines used to shade an area (for example the three districts governed by the Yamashiro league of 1485 to 1493) come from the same CODH dataset: 『旧国・旧郡境界データセット』（CODH作成）「幕末明治地勢地図境界データ」（人間文化研究機構作成）を加工, doi:10.20676/00000454, CC BY-NC 4.0. The line of the Kanmon strait and the street lines bounding the Honnō-ji block are derived from OpenStreetMap data, © OpenStreetMap contributors (ODbL).

Gazetteers used for coordinates

Positions taken from the CODH gazetteers of historical place names are credited: 『日本歴史地名大系』地名項目データセット（CODH作成）doi:10.20676/00000448, and 『日本歴史地名大系』施設・地点項目データセット（CODH作成）doi:10.20676/00000456, both CC BY 4.0. Some positions come from the place-name search of the Geospatial Information Authority of Japan (国土地理院).

Historical (old) map overlays

The draped Genroku (1690), Sekisui (1779) and Ōedo (1860) maps are georeferenced scans from the Ritsumeikan University "Map Warper" project (mapwarper.h-gis.jp), which aligns historical maps to modern coordinates.
