Glossary

The key terms, titles and institutions of premodern Japan, the words behind the map. 53 entries.

tennō天皇

The emperor of Japan, regarded as the sovereign and ritual head of state descended from the sun goddess Amaterasu. For most of premodern history the emperor reigned but did not rule, real power lying with regents, retired emperors, or military leaders.

kuge公家

The civil court aristocracy of Kyoto who served the imperial court and held ceremonial offices and ranks. Distinct from the warrior class (buke), they retained prestige and cultural influence even after political power passed to the samurai.

sesshō摂政

A regent who governed on behalf of an emperor who was a child or otherwise unable to rule. The office was monopolized for centuries by the Fujiwara family.

kampaku関白

A chief adviser and regent acting on behalf of an adult emperor, the civil counterpart of the sesshō. Like the sesshō it was dominated by the Fujiwara; Toyotomi Hideyoshi later held the title as a non-Fujiwara.

shōgun将軍

Short for sei-i taishōgun ("barbarian-subduing great general"), the supreme military commander who, from 1192, was the de facto ruler of Japan under nominal imperial authority. The office was held in turn by the Minamoto, Ashikaga, and Tokugawa houses.

bakufu幕府

Literally "tent government," the military government headed by the shōgun, often rendered "shogunate." Japan had three: the Kamakura (1192–1333), Muromachi/Ashikaga (1336–1573), and Tokugawa/Edo (1603–1868).

shikken執権

The regent who actually controlled the Kamakura shogunate while the shōgun served as a figurehead. The post was held hereditarily by the Hōjō family.

shugo守護

A military governor appointed by the shogunate to oversee one or more provinces, responsible for policing, military levies, and enforcing shogunal authority. Over time many shugo built independent regional power bases.

jitō地頭

A land steward appointed by the shogunate to manage estates (shōen), collect taxes, and maintain order at the local level. Jitō were a key instrument by which the warrior government extended control over the land.

samurai / bushi侍／武士

The hereditary warrior class of premodern Japan. "Bushi" is the broad term for warriors, while "samurai" originally meant those who served a lord; both came to denote the military aristocracy that dominated society from the late Heian period to 1868.

daimyō大名

A great territorial lord who held extensive lands and commanded a body of samurai retainers. Under the Tokugawa, daimyō ruled domains (han) assessed at 10,000 koku or more and were subordinate to the shōgun.

ashigaru足軽

Light-armed foot soldiers, typically of peasant origin, who formed the infantry of warrior armies. Their numbers and use of pikes and matchlock guns grew decisively in the Sengoku period.

rōnin浪人

A masterless samurai who had lost or left the service of a lord, through the lord's death, defeat, or dismissal. Many became wanderers, mercenaries, or sources of social unrest, especially in the early Edo period.

sōhei僧兵

Armed warrior-monks attached to powerful Buddhist temples and monasteries such as Enryaku-ji and Kōfuku-ji. They formed temple militias that could exert military and political pressure, even on the capital.

ikki一揆

A league or uprising, ranging from sworn alliances of warriors or villagers to large armed revolts. Notable forms include peasant tax revolts (do-ikki) and the religiously organized Ikkō-ikki of the Jōdo Shinshū sect.

seppuku切腹

Ritual suicide by cutting the abdomen, performed by samurai to preserve honor, atone for failure, avoid capture, or as a sentence of death. Also colloquially called harakiri, it was governed by strict ceremony, often with an assistant who beheaded the participant.

kokudaka石高

The assessed productive value of a piece of land expressed in koku of rice, used to gauge the wealth, tax obligation, and rank of domains and retainers. The system underpinned landholding and stipends throughout the Edo period.

koku石

A unit of volume of about 180 liters, nominally the amount of rice needed to feed one person for a year. It served as the standard measure of agricultural yield, stipends, and a daimyō's status.

han藩

A domain governed by a daimyō under the Tokugawa shogunate, the basic unit of regional administration. Each han had a degree of internal autonomy while owing allegiance and obligations to the shōgun.

sankin-kōtai参勤交代

The Tokugawa system of "alternate attendance" requiring daimyō to spend alternating periods in residence at the shōgun's capital of Edo and in their domains. Their families were kept in Edo as effective hostages, and the costs of travel and dual residences curbed daimyō wealth and power.

yamashiro / yamajiro山城

A mountain castle built on a hilltop or ridge, relying on steep natural terrain for defense. This was the dominant fortress type during the medieval and early Sengoku eras.

hirajiro平城

A castle built on flat, low-lying ground, typically in or near a town and defended by moats, walls, and ramparts rather than terrain. Such castles became common in the late Sengoku and Edo periods as administrative centers.

mizujiro水城

A castle sited on or beside water, a lake, river, sea, or marsh, using the water as a moat and defensive barrier. The term is sometimes treated as a subtype of the flatland (hirajiro) castle.

hirayamajiro平山城

A "flatland-mountain" castle built on an isolated hill or low rise above a plain, combining the defensibility of a hilltop with proximity to towns and roads. Many of Japan's great Sengoku and Edo castles, such as Himeji, are of this type.

tenshu天守

The central keep or main tower of a Japanese castle, the tallest structure and a symbol of a lord's authority. It served as a watchtower and last redoubt, though daily administration usually took place in surrounding buildings.

Sengoku jidai戦国時代

The "Warring States period," roughly 1467–1568 (extending to 1600 in broader usage), an era of near-constant civil war following the collapse of Ashikaga authority. Regional daimyō fought for supremacy until unification was achieved by Oda Nobunaga, Toyotomi Hideyoshi, and Tokugawa Ieyasu.

gekokujō下剋上

Literally "the lower overthrowing the higher," the social upheaval of the Sengoku era in which retainers, lesser warriors, or commoners displaced their superiors. It typified the period's collapse of established hierarchies.

kamikaze神風

The "divine winds," the typhoons that wrecked the Mongol invasion fleets of 1274 and 1281 and saved Japan from conquest. The events fostered a belief that Japan was a divinely protected land; the term was later revived for World War II suicide pilots.

Bakumatsu幕末

The "end of the shogunate," the turbulent final years of Tokugawa rule (roughly 1853–1868) opened by the arrival of Commodore Perry's American fleet. It saw foreign pressure, domestic political crisis, and the movements that led to the Meiji Restoration.

sonnō jōi尊王攘夷

The political slogan "revere the emperor, expel the barbarians," rallying anti-Tokugawa and anti-foreign activists during the Bakumatsu. It championed restoring imperial authority and resisting Western intrusion, and it helped galvanize the movement to overthrow the shogunate.

taisei hōkan大政奉還

The "return of political power," the formal restoration of governing authority to the emperor by the last shōgun, Tokugawa Yoshinobu, in 1867. It ended the Tokugawa shogunate and paved the way for the Meiji Restoration.

ōsei fukko王政復古

The "restoration of imperial rule," proclaimed in January 1868, which abolished the shogunate and regency offices and declared direct rule by the emperor. It inaugurated the Meiji government that began modernizing Japan.

Meiji Ishin明治維新

The Meiji Restoration of 1868, the political revolution that ended the Tokugawa shogunate and restored the emperor as head of a centralized state. It launched Japan's rapid modernization and is conventionally taken to close the premodern era.

katanagari刀狩

The "sword hunt," Toyotomi Hideyoshi's 1588 policy of confiscating weapons from peasants, monks, and commoners. Together with the cadastral surveys it sharpened the legal divide between the warrior class and the farming population.

garan伽藍

The formal layout of a temple compound and the names of its parts, which recur through the temple entries and their photographs. The sanmon is the great gate at the entrance; the kondō, or golden hall, is the main hall holding the principal image; the tō is the pagoda, a tower of an odd number of storeys, usually three or five, descended from the stupa, the Indian burial mound, and often built over a relic. Arrangement varied by sect and age: excavation at Asuka-dera found three golden halls set around one pagoda, while Zen temples such as Engaku-ji follow Chinese models, the main buildings in a line up a slope.

honmaru / ninomaru本丸 / 二の丸

The walled enclosures, or maru, that a castle is built up from; the atlas entries render them as baileys. The honmaru is the innermost, holding the keep and the lord's palace, while the ninomaru and sannomaru lie beyond it, ringing it on level ground or stepping down the ridge on a hilltop site. Each enclosure has its own wall and moat, so that an attacker had to take them one at a time. The scale is easy to underestimate: Marunouchi, the district around Tokyo Station, is named for the ground within the maru of Edo Castle.

yagura櫓

A turret set on a castle's wall, usually at a corner or beside a gate, serving at once as watchtower, firing position and store for weapons, salt and other supplies. A castle had many yagura but at most one keep, and some had turrets and no keep at all: at Sendai the keep platform was built and then left empty, and four three storey turrets did the work instead. Turrets also outlived keeps. Kumamoto's Uto yagura came through the fire of 1877, so it stands as an Edo period original while the keep shown on the map is a 1960 reconstruction.

insei院政

Cloistered rule, the practice by which an emperor abdicated and then governed from retirement, free of the ceremonial duties of the throne and of the Fujiwara regents who controlled it. Retired Emperor Shirakawa began it by passing the throne to his eight year old son at the turn of 1086 into 1087, and for roughly a century after that the working centre of the court was the retired emperor's household rather than the palace. It was a cloistered emperor, Go-Shirakawa, who granted Yoritomo his shugo and jitō in 1185, an event the atlas pins in Kyoto. The word insei is itself a later label, coined in the Edo period.

Jōdo / Jōdo Shinshū浄土／浄土真宗

Pure Land Buddhism holds that the age is too corrupt for people to save themselves, and rebirth in Amida Buddha's paradise comes instead through trust, expressed by reciting his name in the formula Namu Amida Butsu, the practice called nembutsu. Hōnen made that recitation the sole practice; his pupil Shinran founded Jōdo Shinshū, the True Pure Land school, in which the words are not a payment but thanks for salvation already given. Under Rennyo its congregations became the Ikkō-ikki, who ruled Kaga from 1488 to 1580 and held Ishiyama Hongan-ji against Nobunaga for ten years. The three Hongan-ji on the map belong to this school.

Kannon観音

The bodhisattva of compassion and the most widely worshipped bodhisattva in Japan. A bodhisattva is one who has reached enlightenment but stays in the world to help others, and Kannon is worshipped in many forms, among them the thousand-armed Senju and the horse-headed Batō, who protects animals and travellers. Kannon halls and images stand at Tendai, Shingon, Hossō and Zen temples alike on this map, from Kiyomizu-dera to Ginkaku-ji. The Saigoku pilgrimage, the oldest in Japan, links thirty-three Kannon temples across the Kansai region; the number comes from the thirty-three forms the Lotus Sutra gives the bodhisattva.

Nanto rokushū南都六宗

The six scholarly traditions of Buddhism kept by the state at Nara before the court moved to Kyoto in 794: Sanron, Jōjitsu, Kusha, Hossō, Kegon and Ritsu. They were academic lineages rather than popular movements, and one monk might study several at once. Four label pins on this map: Kegon at Tōdai-ji, Ritsu at Tōshōdai-ji, Sanron at Asuka-dera and Hossō at Kōfuku-ji, Yakushi-ji, Hōryū-ji and Kiyomizu-dera. Ritsu takes its name from the vinaya, the rule governing monks, and existed to ordain them lawfully, which is why envoys begged the Chinese master Ganjin to cross the sea, and why these rich old houses fought later reformers.

Nichiren-shū日蓮宗

The school founded by the monk Nichiren in the thirteenth century, which holds that the Lotus Sutra alone carries the truth and that chanting its title, Namu Myōhō Renge Kyō, is the whole of practice. Nichiren condemned the rival schools by name, and in a treatise of 1260 he warned that the country's error would bring civil war and foreign invasion; the Mongol fleets came fourteen years later. That stand twice earned him exile and in 1271 nearly cost him his head. It is the only major Japanese school named after its founder, and the atlas pins his Kamakura hermitage site, Myōhō-ji.

Shingon真言

The esoteric school of Buddhism founded by Kūkai after his study in Tang China; the name means "true word" and refers to the mantras at the centre of its practice. Esoteric here means that the teaching passes from master to pupil through initiation, hand gestures, chanted formulas and mandala paintings rather than through public preaching, so it cannot be learned from books alone. Its mountain headquarters, Kongōbu-ji on Mount Kōya, and its capital seat, Tōji in Kyoto, are both pinned on the map, along with Daigo-ji, Ninna-ji and the militant New Shingon centre at Negoro-ji.

Tendai天台

The Buddhist school Saichō founded on Mount Hiei, northeast of Kyoto, after studying in China; the court recognised it in 806. It teaches that the Lotus Sutra carries the whole of the Buddha's message and that enlightenment is open to everyone. Its headquarters, Enryaku-ji, grew into the largest religious institution in the country and raised the warrior monks who repeatedly marched on the capital, and almost every founder of the later Kamakura schools, Hōnen, Shinran, Dōgen and Nichiren among them, trained there first. Eight temples on the map are Tendai, among them Mii-dera, Chūson-ji and Rinnō-ji at Nikkō.

Zen / Rinzai / Sōtō禅 / 臨済 / 曹洞

A school of Buddhism that treats seated meditation, zazen, as the heart of practice rather than scripture or ritual. It reached Japan in two branches: Rinzai, brought home from China by Eisai, which sets pupils baffling questions called kōan to break ordinary thinking, and Sōtō, brought by Dōgen, who held that sitting is not a method for reaching enlightenment but enlightenment itself. Zen won the patronage of the warrior class and shaped ink painting, gardens and the tea gathering. The atlas pins seven Rinzai temples, among them Kenchō-ji and Kinkaku-ji, and two Sōtō houses, Eihei-ji and Sōji-ji.

gozan五山

The "Five Mountains", the official ranking of Rinzai Zen monasteries kept first by the Kamakura shogunate and then by the Ashikaga, with a separate list of five for Kyoto and for Kamakura. Rank brought patronage and duty together: Gozan monks served the shogunate as diplomats, translators and China traders, and their monasteries became the leading centres of learning and Chinese poetry in the country. Five temples on the map sit in this hierarchy, Kenchō-ji and Engaku-ji standing first and second in Kamakura, Tenryū-ji and Shōkoku-ji first and second in Kyoto, and Nanzen-ji above both lists in a class of its own.

honji suijaku / shinbutsu shūgō本地垂迹 / 神仏習合

Shinbutsu shūgō is the fusion of kami worship and Buddhism into a single religious world; honji suijaku is the doctrine that held it together, teaching that the kami of Japan were local traces of buddhas and bodhisattvas, so that worshipping the one meant worshipping the other. For most of premodern history shrines and temples shared sites, staff and income, until the Meiji government forced them apart in 1868. This is why the atlas keeps temples and shrines on a single layer, and why the three gilded buddhas of Rinnō-ji at Nikkō are understood as the Buddhist form of the mountain kami worshipped next door at Futarasan Shrine.

kami神

The gods or spirits of the Shinto tradition, resident in mountains, rivers, trees, unusual places, powerful ancestors and sometimes in dead emperors and warlords. A shrine is built to house a kami rather than an image of one; the kami is present in a sacred object kept in the honden, the inner hall at the rear, which is normally closed to visitors and entered only by priests. The eight shrines on this map are dedicated to kami of very different kinds, from the sun goddess Amaterasu at Ise to Tokugawa Ieyasu, enshrined at Nikkō in 1617, the year after he died.

mappō末法

The Buddhist doctrine of the Latter Day of the Law, the last of three ages after the Buddha's death, in which the teaching still survives but almost no one can practise it or reach enlightenment by their own effort. Japanese reckoning placed the start of this age in 1052, and the belief spread quickly among aristocrats and monks just as order was breaking down in the provinces. It is the anxiety behind the Pure Land movement, with its trust in Amida rather than in one's own striving, and behind the Byōdō-in at Uji, converted from a villa into a temple in that very year and laid out as an image of Amida's paradise.

Noh / sarugaku能 / 猿楽

Noh is the slow, masked, poetic drama that the actor Kan'ami and his son Zeami built in the late fourteenth century out of sarugaku, a rough popular entertainment of comic sketches, dance and mime performed at temples and shrines; the two are explained together here because one grew out of the other. The patronage of the shōgun Ashikaga Yoshimitsu turned a players' craft into a courtly art, and its plots come largely from the Tale of the Heike and from classical poetry, so many plays are set at places this atlas maps. A Noh stage has stood out over the sea at Itsukushima Shrine since 1590.

chanoyu茶の湯

The tea gathering, a formally ordered meeting at which a host prepares and serves powdered green tea to a few guests in a small room. Sen no Rikyū, tea master first to Oda Nobunaga and then to Toyotomi Hideyoshi, gave it the aesthetic known as wabi: a preference for the plain, the worn and the imperfect over the costly and the new. Tea was also politics, since Hideyoshi used these gatherings to bind allies to him, and in 1591 he ordered Rikyū to take his own life.

renga and haiku連歌 / 俳句

Linked verse, a chain of stanzas of seventeen and fourteen syllables composed by turns by several poets sitting together, and the favourite literary game of Muromachi courtiers and warlords alike. The two terms share an entry because the second grew out of the first: the opening stanza of a renga, called the hokku, came in time to be written and read on its own, and that standalone verse is what we now call haiku. Matsuo Bashō (1644 to 1694) made it a serious art of season, place and the passing moment, and the atlas draws the road of his travel diary Oku no Hosomichi across the north.

ukiyo-e浮世絵

"Pictures of the floating world", the woodblock prints of Edo, sold cheaply to townspeople and showing at first courtesans, kabuki actors and the pleasure districts. The phrase puns on an older Buddhist word for a world of sorrow that does not last. Full-colour printing arrived in the 1760s, and these brocade pictures, nishiki-e 錦絵, gave the form the look it is known by today; several battles in this atlas are illustrated by such prints, made centuries after the fighting. Later artists widened the subject, Hokusai, pinned here among the people, turning it toward landscape and ordinary work.
